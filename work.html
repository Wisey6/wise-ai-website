Automation case study

From paper to a business that increasingly runs itself.

A multi-attraction family entertainment venue in regional Queensland — sixteen lanes of bowling, an arcade, a café, birthday parties, leagues — run hands-on by its two owners, Peter and Jeanette. They'd been at it long enough to know exactly what they wanted: to be down to two days a week within three years.

Everything ran on paper, spreadsheets and memory. I spent a weekend on site walking every attraction and every back-office desk, ranked what I found, and started with the lowest-risk, highest-value work first.

5 hrs → 30 minThe weekly roster, on the owners' own estimate, once Claude drafts it from their template.
30 min → 0Per party booking. A phone call, a form, a deposit chase and four systems, now one form the customer fills in.
2 days / wkThe goal the whole engagement is sequenced toward, within three years.
How I got to the list
Step 1 · Understand
Interview, observe, then prioritise

One-on-one with the owners about their goals and their friction. Then with the staff doing the actual work, to find the real bottlenecks. Then watching the systems run live, to see where data moved and where it got re-typed by hand. Nearly every answer looped back to the same wish: "I wish all the data lived in one place."

Step 2 · Score
Rank every workflow on how automatable it is

Each job scored on how much time it eats, how repeatable it is, how structured its data is, and how much it would cost to get wrong. That turned "everything's a problem" into an ordered list of what to build first, and what to deliberately leave to a person.

What's running
Rostering, drafted by Claude Live

Before: an Excel template rebuilt from scratch every week, staff texting their availability on Sunday night, all of it hand-transferred into Google Calendar and then into Deputy. About five hours a week.

Now: Peter opens one place, says "build next week's roster," and gets a draft in their own template — built from a staff master, their award rules, upcoming parties from the booking system, and his own calendar. He reviews it, adjusts it in plain English, and enters it into Deputy himself. Deliberately: Claude drafts, Peter decides, and the tool never touches pay. That's their requirement, and it's why it's safe.

Party bookings, self-serve Live

Before: a discovery call, a paper form, a $100 deposit to chase, lanes booked in one system, laser tag in a spreadsheet, the summary sheet updated for the roster, a party sheet printed. Thirty minutes each, by hand.

Now: the customer fills in one form, sees live pricing, pays the deposit through Stripe, and the booking lands in the roster automatically. Next.js on Vercel, a Supabase database underneath, real deposits since August. The old paper form is retired.

Upstairs timer Delivered

The venue has rooms upstairs — karaoke and spaces out of sight of the front counter — and when a session ran over nobody noticed until the next group was waiting. A small timer app now tracks every room, so staff know when to check people out and nobody is forgotten.

FAQ assistant on the website In progress

An assistant that answers the repeat questions and captures after-hours enquiries, running on the venue's own account with a hard monthly cap.

One dashboard Next

Bowling, arcade and café takings stitched into one view with a plain-English note when something dips. The "all the data in one place" wish, answered.

Every piece runs on the venue's own accounts and tools, lowest-risk first, each one handing a little more of the repetitive work to the system and a little more of the week back to the owners. They keep every account. Nothing routes through me.
